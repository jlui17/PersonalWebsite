# Sprites

The pixel sprites of justinlui.dev: Justin in his straw hat (`justin.js`), Truffle his French bulldog (`truffle.js`), his two agents as robots (`luibot.js`, `luibuilder.js`), and the props (`props.js`; its default export lists them). `Sprite.jsx` draws them. `/?design=sprites` shows every frame of every action, zoomed and at true size.

What the sprites are for, and what Justin wants from them in a design, is in `AGENTS.md` under "Sprites". This file is the craft: Part 1 is how to draw and change a sheet, Part 2 is how to stage sprites in a design. Every rule here came from a rejection of his or from a reviewer finding whose fix held. His words are quoted as he dictated them, typos included. "The coordinator" is defined in `AGENTS.md`.

## Part 1. Drawing and changing a sheet

### How a sheet is built

- A frame is an array of strings of equal length, one character per pixel. `palette` maps a character to a colour and `.` is transparent. `outfits` are palette overrides on the same frames: the hoodie is the over-shirt frames recoloured, with hood cells that are `null` by day. A garment with a new shape has to be drawn. It cannot be a recolour.
- `actions[name] = { frames, interval, durations?, stride? }`. `durations` (ms per frame) wins over `interval`. `Sprite` restarts at frame 0 when the action changes, holds frame 0 under `prefers-reduced-motion`, and shows one fixed frame when the caller passes `frame`.
- One sprite pixel is one CSS pixel, and designs render at `scale={1}`. `Sprite` defaults to `scale={3}`, so always pass `scale={1}`. He asked for sprites "larger in terms of pixels" that "keep them at their current size" on the page, so the detail went up and the size did not. Side views face right; pass `flip` for the other way. `flip` is a transform inside the svg, so a caller's own CSS `transform` still applies.
- A new sheet must be added to the `characters` object in `Sprite.jsx` or nothing can render it. Keep an export until nothing imports it, also in the round that replaces it, and render the page after every save: `props.js` saved without two props that `Sprite.jsx` imported blanked his dev page.
- **The comment above each action is the staging contract**: the canvas size, where the body sits in it, where a partner's canvas goes, which is painted first, and which frame hands off to which action. Designs read those numbers from there. When you redraw an action, update its comment in the same change.
- **One artist owns a sheet at a time, and regenerates it from a workbench** (a build script over hand-drawn parts). This is the coordinator's working rule. No workbench is in this repo, whatever the sheet headers say, so edit rows in place and keep every row of a frame the same length.

### Method

1. **The artist does not pass their own art, and neither does whoever wrote the brief.** His instruction: "for the parts u design, use subagent reviewers to get fresh perspecgtives". The incidents behind it, and how a review runs, are in `docs/design-review.md`.
2. **Judge at true size first, then zoomed (8x and 16x), on the light page and on a dark page, as stills and in motion.** His first complaint only showed at true size: "it looks decent when it's full size, but when it's like shrink down to fit on the website" the outline "makes it just seem a little off". His later ones (the lipstick, the four arms, the giraffe) were made zoomed in. Ears that grow mid-stride, a pat that never lifts and a ball that skids do not show in stills. A pose that reads at 8x can be a dark blob at 1x.
3. **After two failed patches to one feature, redesign the whole part.** This is the "After two failed patches" rule in `docs/design-process.md` ("Showing him work"), applied to art. For a sprite it means research first, then several whole candidates, then pick. The side head was rejected four times ("the bottom of my head looks too skinny"; "TOO round" with hair like an "afro"; "horrendous"; a mouth that read as lipstick) and failed two more reviews, because each fix to one feature broke its neighbour. Six candidates that differed only in mouth and eye cells could not pass, since the chin, jaw and hair were the problem; a full redraw from research passed. After three rejected mouths he asked "can u do some research for inspiration on pixel art mouths", and the research found the cause in one step (the mouth was in outline ink, as heavy as the eyes). Before the redraw, write down every wrong read so far (lipstick, pout, muzzle, bob) so that none can come back.
4. **Brief with goals, not pixel geometry.** Say what the pose must read as, what it must not read as, and the hard constraints (canvas, shared head, outline rule). Four over-tight briefs each produced worse art: "1 to 2px of hair outside the skull" gave a bald back of the head; "no head cell may change" gave a pole arm with a fist above the hat; "eyes just visible over the top" of the carried boxes gave a masked face; a reviewer's "raised elbow and a steep forearm" was built exactly and failed the next review as a bracket. A reviewer's sketch is a minimum to improve on, never something to copy. Pasted in as drawn, reviewer sketches failed the next review twice.
5. **When the same kind of defect comes back, fix it in the structure and add an automated check.** One pose had a different head in three reviews in a row, until every front standing frame was built from one head block and every side frame from the walk's head. The hand audit passed a frame with no hand until it got an expected count per frame and a minimum blob size; the repaired audit then found a second defect.
6. **Small rounds, then a diff against the last version he saw.** After "cool sprites look pretty good", the bar for a polish round is that nothing else moved. Cap the rounds and go back to him with a choice when the cap is hit. The cap used was five; this is the coordinator's practice.

### The checks

Run `node src/sprites/check.mjs` after every round and paste its output into the report. The comment at the top of the script lists what it checks. Its data block (the head allow-lists and the arm pieces) was written by a workbench that is not in this repo, so when you redraw a frame of Justin, edit that frame's rows in the block by hand. A frame that is not listed there is not checked for its head or its arm.

The script does not run these checks yet. Do them by hand until someone adds them:

- Every row of a frame has the same length, every frame of an action has the same size, and every character is in the palette.
- **Closure, holes and sealed cells** for Truffle, the robots and the props. The script runs these three on `justin` and `desk` only.
- **Any check at all on a prop the script does not import.** It imports `coffee`, `shoeRack`, `shoeboxes`, `chicken`, `cow` and `sheep`, so the other props in `props.js` get no check, not even "consecutive frames differ".
- **Arm outline thickness**: 2px where an arm meets air, 1px where it crosses another form.
- **Contrast on dark**: every fill of a prop is at least 3:1 against a dark ground.

### Art rules, and the rejection behind each

The exact colours are the named constants at the top of each sheet, with the reason beside each. The outline rule is written out in the header of `truffle.js`.

- **Ink is a near-black warm brown, never pure black. A closed 2px outline wherever a fill meets air, 1px interior lines, and a 1px rim only on declared thin parts** (ears, hands, feet, beak, comb, legs, the steam wand, an antenna light). A 1px outline may step diagonally and the 2px one never does. "the outline is not dark enough ... not having black outline everywhere"; after the fix, "the borders look good tho". Hair must sit far enough off the ink that the two do not fuse at true size.
- **A black line separates every arm from the shirt, in every pose and outfit.** "the distinction between the shirt and the arms is not there ... i'd rather have them have like a black line separation so that you know there are like arms there."
- **Flowy is not fat, and raised arms must not make him skinny.** "i like flowy, but the pants look kind of just like fat" led to a hip flare with a crease line and tapered legs. "on the arms up sprite, my body is a bit too skinny": when a sleeve leaves his side, the torso fill widens.
- **Every pose is the same man.** One shared front head and one shared side head, cell for cell. Side and front agree on hair rows, eye line, chin row and hat size, and the side skull is about as deep as the front face is wide (a shallower one is what he called "too skinny"). The sleep head is still drawn a little smaller. It has only ever been shown alone, and he has not commented.
- **Count the skin blobs in every frame**: the face plus the hands the pose shows (idle 3, walk 2, sit 2, sip 3, hat 3, arms-up 3, pet 2). "the arms up sprites have 4 arms now"; a sip frame once had no hand at all.
- **The front smile is a thin, closed, red-brown line with a skin row under the nose, never outline ink.** The eyes stay the darkest thing on the face. He rejected a wide grin ("grinning like a madman"), a small mouth with a centred tooth ("now i just look like a rabbit"), and an ink curve as heavy as the eyes ("the smile still looks weird"). He has seen the current one.
- **No mouth colour on a profile edge.** The side head has a notch mouth in a skin shade. A reviewer prescribed a red-brown side mouth to match the front. On the side-view head it read as lipstick, and he rejected it.
- **The side head is a short textured crop**: "my hair should also look like a textured fringe hairstyle so there should be waves and texture." The hair ends at the bottom of the ear with nape skin showing, the back silhouette has notches, highlights are short staggered strokes (never single pixels, one long sweep or parallel hatching), the nose tip is the most forward cell, the chin sits behind the nose, and the neck carries the head. Earlier versions were read as a bald back, a helmet, a bob, a pout, a muzzle and a duck bill.
- **The hat is centred on the skull in every view.** "on the side profile sprite, the hat is not centred with the top of my head".
- **Nothing thin next to bare skin may be skin-coloured.** "the hoodie shoe straings should have a more distinct visual than my skin": the drawstrings are a cool off-white with ink tips and start under a collar line. In the same family, an earring pixel at mouth height read as a cigarette.
- **A raised or reaching arm has a shoulder, a real corner at the elbow or one plain diagonal, and ends in a hand on the thing.** Never a vertical post and never a right-angle L. The hat-tip arm read as a flagpole for several rounds, and the petting arm read as a bracket or a crane. A hand on the brim leaves straw visible past it, or the hat looks lopsided. A reaching hand is open, like the wave: a pointing one got "it looks like i stick out a single finger".
- **An arm has one length in every pose.** The hanging arm in `idle` is the reference. "when i wave my arm is way too long compared to when it's by my side, it should remain consistent."
- **A raised arm joins the shoulder in fill, not only in ink.** On a dark page the ink disappears, and the raised arm of the hoodie `hat` pose floated beside his head: "my arm is basically not connected to my body lol". Check every raised arm on the dark page at true size.
- **Truffle reads as a French bulldog at true size, and everything that says Frenchie is in the head**: bat ears taller than wide with a visible pink inside, a flat broad face, wide-set eyes, a grey chin, no tail, a level back and a barrel body. She read as a bear, then a fox, then a cat, then a boar. "make her a little bit chubby"; "give truffle some depth to her belly". Her coat needs enough contrast off the ink for the shading to show, and it must differ from Justin's hair. Truffle is female.
- **An animal reaches the ground by bending its body, never by growing a neck.** "to reach the ground, both the chicken and the cow turn into a giraffe"; his own suggestion was "maybe the animals need to bend down in their front legs and neck so we dont have to stretch as much?". The cow's front drops at the shoulder and her front legs shorten and splay. The hen tips like a seesaw. The sheep slides her face down the fleece. An in-between frame is a true midpoint: a hen frame that reached the ground before the body had moved was the giraffe again in miniature.
- **The robots are family.** They wear Justin's exact hat and clothes colours, one robot per outfit, and are told apart as blue against orange (the colour rule is in `AGENTS.md`). "they should still look like robots but" also "have some swag"; "add the straw hat to both bots". Attitude comes from asymmetry (luibot's cocked hat, luibuilder's hand on a stone block), and a prop that vanishes at true size is cut (a pencil read as a yellow flag).
- **Feet and ground contact are on the bottom row of every frame, and the body stays at the same canvas position across a character's actions.** A design can then switch actions with no jump. Spare rows go at the top, for the walk bob, the hat lift and the ear prick.
- **Every part reads by its fills on a dark page.** The ink outline disappears on a dark ground. "the desk legs blend in with the background ... so it looks like a floating desk i want it to have legs"; the steam wand vanished the same way. Thin parts get a steel or tan core, nothing is drawn in ink alone, and each fill is 3:1 or better against the dark ground.
- **A prop carries its own context and scale.** The coffee corner stands on its own hip-high cabinet; on a bare slab it read as a vending machine. It shows real gear, because he is specific: the Bambino Plus has its steam wand off the side wall (as a handle it read as a kettle) and the Baratza Encore has its flared clear hopper. Boxes in a stack are offset or they read as a dresser. A comb is serrated or it reads as a little hat. No invented markings, such as a white chest patch on Truffle.

### Motion

- **Legs never go in together and out together.** "it looks a little silly when like both legs go in and both legs go out ... i don't think that's how people will like walk or how dogs walk." A biped goes contact, passing, contact, passing, with the far leg a step darker and behind, the arms counter-swinging, and a one-row bob on the passing frames. A quadruped walks a lateral-sequence four-beat walk: near hind, near front, far hind, far front, a quarter cycle apart. A two-beat trot makes the near legs scissor, which is exactly what he called silly, so `trot` is for fast travel only.
- **Each planted foot moves back exactly `stride` px per frame, and the sheet exports `stride`.** A design advances one frame per `stride` px of travel and never runs a walk on a timer while moving at another speed. One prototype drove him at several times the speed the cycle was drawn for and his feet skated.
- **Rigid things stay rigid.** Head, ears and neck move with the body as one block. Truffle's ears once grew by two rows every other frame, and shading that changes its cell count between frames flickers.
- **The ball's spin is read from its position, not from a clock**: one turn per circumference, with the turns rounded so it stops on the rest frame. "the tennis ball should animate a roll as it rolls to truffle"; the first version ticked on a timer under an eased travel, so it skidded at the start and spun in place at the end.
- **Use per-frame `durations`.** A blink is about 160ms shut and seconds open; a flat interval made him doze. Idle is a blink, not a hop: a 2px whole-body hop read as a render glitch. A play-once action holds its last frame with a very long duration.
- **A gesture must be visible at true size.** A 2 to 3px hand wiggle is a twitch, not a wave. A pat needs a row of air under the hand in the lifted frame, and the lift comes from the arm pivoting, not from the forearm getting shorter. A 1px nudge of a prop reads as jitter.

## Part 2. Staging sprites in a design

- **Paint order between two overlapping sprites depends on the state, and lives in one table per design.** Petting a sitting Truffle, she is painted over him, so her near ear is in front of his forearm. Petting her asleep, he is painted over her, so his hand is on her back. The pose failed three reviews while the rule was "he is always on top". At the coffee corner the machine is painted first and his arm goes over it. When the order changes, change every place that paints the pair in the same commit.
- **Place a partner's canvas by the offsets in the sheet comment, on a shared ground row.** The comments above `pet`, `pet-low`, `press` and `grab` in `justin.js` give them. If he is flipped, take the offset from his other edge. The offsets change whenever the art is redrawn, so read them in one place.
- **A touch is a touch: the hand overlaps the thing, and the thing reacts on the contact frame.** "my hands are not actually on the chair"; at the status switch, "it kind of looks like he's just waving his hand and then the toggle gets flipped". The art was right both times and the staging was wrong: he stood 22px left of the chair back, luibot pushed the chair 14px too high, and luibot hovered 18px above the knob, placed from the wrong rows of his sheet, while the sign lit after his arm was down. So check staging, then timing, then art. To place a contact, take the hand's cells for that frame from the comment above the action (luibot's `fly-wave`: cols 0-8, rows 5-13), mirror them when the figure faces left, put the figure so those cells land on the thing, and fire the effect on that frame (`done` in a `reach` of `flyToSpot`). Measure the gap on the real page; the art strip proves nothing about staging. After fixing one contact, sweep the others for the same arithmetic: the chair pull, luibot's push, the switch, the feed, the cup.
- **Ground lines to the pixel.** The svg's bottom edge equals the rule's top edge, measured with `getBoundingClientRect` at 1280 and 375. Misses so far: a robot centred on a label floated a few px; a generic `top: 0` beat `bottom: 0` and lifted the robots 42px; fractional rows put props a fraction of a px into a hairline (round the y); a rule cut short as a joke read as a bug.
- **Whole-number scale, no smoothing, and never scale a sprite to fit.** If the sprite towers over a phone heading, change the type or the layout.
- **Reserve the box for the largest pose the figure can take there, and anchor it at the bottom and one side.** Canvases differ between actions, so anchor by the body position given in the sheet comment (the `wave`, `press` and `grab` bodies are shifted inside wider canvases), not by the canvas edge. Misses so far: a cheer taken from the wrong action's height sank him 10px into a widget; a wide sleeping pose clipped off a phone's left edge.
- **The design steps a one-shot action; it never lets a loop play for a guessed time.** Pressing a button or taking a cup happens once, so the design drives it with `Sprite`'s `frame` prop and its own timers, from a script of steps (action, frame, prop state, ms). `Sprite` loops every action and restarts at frame 0 when the action changes, so a timed loop drifts. A looped coffee sequence pressed the button twice and showed two cups, then none. A hand-off frame matches the next action cell for cell (the last `grab` frame equals the first `sip` frame), so the cut does not show. **A one-shot ends in a pose even when its follow-up is dropped, and a dropped chore still reports done.** Truffle looped `wake` for good after an interrupted throw, a chore dropped mid-reach left luibot holding a pinned frame, and a chore dropped by an early scroll never lit the sign.
- **Prop state follows the actor's frame: one cup, in one place, in every frame.** The coffee corner goes idle, brew, ready, and then empty on exactly the frame where the cup appears in his hand; putting the cup back plays the frames in reverse. The cup on the tray is pixel-identical to the cup in `sip`. Gate a pose on its partner: he kneels to pet only once Truffle has arrived. There is one ball and one Truffle on a page.
- **The arm nearest the thing does the work.** "sometimes the wrong hand moves to use the coffee. like if the coffee stand is on my left, i move my right arm and vice versa." When he is flipped, the working arm flips with him.
- **Furniture stays put, and he walks to it.** "i feel like the desk should remain at step 7 and then i walk to it and sit down in it" (step 6 today). A desk, a coffee corner or a table is there whether he is or not.
- **A hover area must not change under the pointer.** Truffle once stuck awake after a fast mouse pass: her svg was swapped for a canvas of another size, the `pointerout` went to the detached node, and React never saw the leave. Make a persistent button with a fixed box the hit target, and give the svg `pointer-events: none`. React to hover, to keyboard focus-visible and to tap (toggle on `click`, so a scroll that starts on a sprite is not a tap, and a tap elsewhere releases). Do not lift the container on hover when the sprite's own reaction is 1px. Do not disable a button mid-animation, or focus falls to `body`.
- **Reduced motion: everything is still and the page is still complete.** Walkers are already where they were going, the ball is at its end point, a script shows its end state and none of the poses on the way (run through quickly, they read as a slideshow), a loop holds frame 0. `Sprite` alone holds frame 0, so a play-once gesture the design does not step shows its first frame (the hat not yet tipped).
- **Time of day drives the pose and the outfit, and `?hour=0..23` makes it checkable by hand.** `justin.js` holds the outfits and `index.jsx` holds the hour table. Each part of the day needs a visibly different pose; a recolour alone is not a change. Check the odd pairings (he once sipped espresso at 2am). Night is what visitors in Europe and Asia get by default, so night still needs something to do. The hour is one of the ways into a scene; the full list is in `docs/design-process.md` ("Checking a design by hand").
- **Nothing vanishes or appears from nowhere, and a prop drawn inside an action's art exists only while that action plays.** The boxes are part of `carry`, so they vanished for the 2.6 s greeting wave at step 1 and for the cheer, and while the desk was part of `type` a cheer made it flash. Furniture gets its own sheet (`desk`). Check every new pose or gesture against each state where he holds or sits in something: he keeps the holding pose, or the prop waits somewhere in view (the boxes stay on the rack until his wave is over). The test case is the search palette's "Episode 1070" command (press `/`), which calls `cheer()` in `stage.js`: both arms go up for 1.8 s, and he returns to a pixel-identical pose.
- **A robot's beam keeps clear of Justin, and in a pane luibot follows his anchor.** Both rules are in the engine, because a beam drawn over him was fixed in one place and lived in three more. `clearOf` in `stage.js` holds a beam until its box is clear of him and of where his walk can take him. `followAnchor` reads the anchor's place on every step, so a scroll never leaves luibot over the words; when the anchor leaves the pane's window he beams out and the chore reports done. Test a spot that scrolls away: the first clearance rule froze him there for up to 3 s.
- **On a dark page the ink outline has the same value as the ground, so it vanishes and parts joined only by ink float.** The fix in code: `Sprite` paints the outer outline (the ink near empty canvas) with the CSS variable `--sprite-outline`, and a dark design sets it to a soft brown just lighter than its ground, for example `#463d33` on the panes ground `#28221b`. Interior lines, eyes and noses stay ink, and a light page is unchanged. The comment above `isOuterOutline` in `Sprite.jsx` has the details. He has seen it on the live page and has not commented. An outline much lighter than that starts to merge with, or invert against, the darkest fills (his hair, the far shoe, Truffle's shade). The rule above about fills still applies to every part, and his shoes and far leg are hard to see at true size on a dark ground.
- **Verification traps.** `docs/design-process.md` covers frozen tabs ("Checking a design by hand") and one script per headless Chrome ("Shipping to main"). For sprites also: screenshot round-trips inflate frame times, so read the DOM every 12 to 40ms (the svg's rect and its `aria-label`, such as `justin walk`); a viewport resize in a driver can reset a scene; hot reload on a half-saved sheet can blank the page once.
